Units: kilometers to meters

The speed is in meters per second. The distance is in kilometers. Before you multiply or subtract, make the units match.

1 km = 1,000 m
Worked example · practice object, not 2026-XJ

Convert 5.0 × 107 km to meters.

5.0 × 107 km × 1,000 m/km = 5.0 × 107 × 103 m = 5.0 × 1010 m

Shortcut: multiplying by 1,000 adds 3 to the exponent. 7 + 3 = 10.

Part 2 · Tool 3 of 6

Split the velocity into two parts

Velocity is a vector: it has a size and a direction. The asteroid isn't aimed straight at Earth. It's tilted. So we split its velocity into a toward-Earth part and a sideways part. These parts are called components.

vtoward = v × cos θ vside = v × sin θ Memory trick: cos goes with the side that touches the angle.
Worked example · practice object, not 2026-XJ

An object moves at 6,000 m/s, tilted 20° from the line toward Earth.

vtoward = 6,000 × cos 20° = 6,000 × 0.940 = 5,640 m/s vside = 6,000 × sin 20° = 6,000 × 0.342 = 2,050 m/s

Sense check: each part is smaller than 6,000. A part can never be bigger than the whole. Set your calculator to degree mode.

Check yourself · spot the error

A student writes: vtoward = 6,000 × sin 20° = 2,050 m/s. What went wrong?

Part 2 · Tool 4 of 6

Where will it be? xf = xi + vt

When speed stays the same, final position = initial position + (velocity × time).

xffinal position (where it ends up)
xiinitial position ("x initial")
vvelocity (m/s)
ttime (s)

Moving toward Earth makes the distance shrink, so that velocity counts as negative. xf = xi + vt becomes: xf = xi − vtoward × t

Worked example · same practice object, 30 days

Start: 5.0 × 1010 m away. vtoward = 5,640 m/s. t = 2.59 × 106 s.

distance covered = 5,640 × 2.59 × 106 = 1.46 × 1010 m xf = 5.0 × 1010 − 1.46 × 1010 = 3.54 × 1010 m (3.54 × 107 km) sideways = 2,050 × 2.59 × 106 = 5.3 × 109 m off to the side

Think like an analyst. What if the distance covered is bigger than the start distance? Your answer goes negative. That means it has passed Earth's distance. Don't erase it. Explain what it means.

Part 2 · Explainer before Tool 5

What is uncertainty?

Every measurement is a little bit off. Not because someone messed up, but because no tool is perfect. Uncertainty tells you how far off a measurement could be.

You already use uncertainty
YOUR PHONE MAP The circle around your dot The map isn’t sure exactly where you are. You’re somewhere inside that circle.
THE BUS APP "Arriving in 6–10 min" The app can’t give one exact time, so it gives a range.
A RULER Only as good as its marks A ruler marked in millimeters can’t measure anything smaller than a millimeter.
How to read ±
value±uncertainty
Lowest it could be = value − uncertainty Highest it could be = value + uncertainty
Worked examples · from easy to space

The bus is 8 min ± 2 min away.

8 − 2 = 6 min · 8 + 2 = 10 minThe bus will come in 6 to 10 minutes.

The practice object is 5.0 × 107 km ± 40,000 km away.

50,000,000 − 40,000 = 49,960,000 km
50,000,000 + 40,000 = 50,040,000 kmIt is somewhere between 49,960,000 and 50,040,000 km away.
MISTAKE A mistake is wrong Like using the wrong formula. You fix it.
UNCERTAINTY Uncertainty is honest It shows how good your data is. You report it.

Why it matters for 2026-XJ: if NASA’s data were perfect, we could name one exact spot. It isn’t, so we give a range. Telling people that range is part of being honest with them.

Part 2 · Tool 5 of 6

The uncertainty window

NASA's numbers aren't perfect. So we find the closest and farthest it could be. The space between them is the window.

Wiggle 1 · the start Position uncertainty. It stays the same size.
Wiggle 2 · the speed Velocity uncertainty × time. It grows every second.
Worked example · same practice object, 30 days

Position ± 40,000 km. Velocity ± 150 m/s. Predicted distance: 3.54 × 107 km.

speed wiggle = 150 × 2.59 × 106 = 3.89 × 108 m = 389,000 km total wiggle = 40,000 + 389,000 = ± 429,000 km window: 3.497 × 107 km to 3.583 × 107 km
closestpredictedfarthest

Big idea: the further ahead we predict, the wider the window.

Part 2 · Honors challenge A

Combine uncertainties two ways

In Tool 5 you added the two wiggles. That is the worst case: both errors at their biggest, in the same direction. That is unlikely. Scientists also use a likely case, called root-sum-square.

Worst casetotal = a + bSafe. Never too small. Can be too big.
Likely case (root-sum-square)total = √(a² + b²)Use when the two errors are independent: one doesn't cause the other.
Worked example · same practice object, 30 days

Wiggle 1 = 40,000 km. Wiggle 2 = 389,000 km.

worst case = 40,000 + 389,000 = ± 429,000 km likely = √(40,000² + 389,000²) = √(1.6 × 109 + 1.51 × 1011) ≈ ± 391,000 km

Notice: the bigger wiggle wins. When one error is much bigger, fix that one first.

In your task: report both. Then say which one you would tell the public, and why.

Part 2 · Honors challenge B

Test the assumption: is "negligible" really negligible?

The task says Earth’s gravity is less than 0.001 m/s², so we ignored it. A strong analyst checks that choice. How far could that tiny pull move the asteroid?

extra distance = ½ × a × t²a = acceleration (m/s²) · t = time (s)
Worked example · same practice object, 30 days

Use the biggest possible pull: a = 0.001 m/s². t = 2.59 × 106 s.

extra = ½ × 0.001 × (2.59 × 106)² = 0.0005 × 6.71 × 1012 = 3.35 × 109 m (3.35 × 106 km)

Compare: the uncertainty window was ± 429,000 km. The gravity effect could be about 8 times bigger than the window.

Why this matters: time is squared. A tiny pull over a long time adds up fast. Constant velocity is a good first estimate, not the final answer. Saying this is exactly what a 4 in Reasoning looks like.

Check yourself

Part 2 · Honors challenge C

How close does it get?

If the asteroid keeps going in a straight line, it doesn’t hit Earth dead-on. It is tilted. The closest approach is where its path passes nearest to Earth.

closest distance = xi × sin θ time to get there = (xi × cos θ) ÷ v The blue dashed line meets the path at a right angle. That is the shortest gap.
Worked example · same practice object

xi = 5.0 × 107 km, θ = 20°, v = 6,000 m/s.

closest = 5.0 × 107 × sin 20° = 5.0 × 107 × 0.342 = 1.71 × 107 km time = (5.0 × 1010 m × 0.940) ÷ 6,000 m/s = 7.83 × 106 s (about 91 days)

Check the units: distance in m ÷ speed in m/s gives seconds.

Connect it: does closest approach happen before or after 6 months for 2026-XJ? That changes what your graph means.

Part 3 · How you're scored

Four scores, each 0 to 4

Your work gets four separate scores. Each one asks a different question.

1 · SCIENCE Is the physics right? A 4 looks like: correct components, xf = xi + vt used right, uncertainty carried through, exact vocabulary.
2 · REASONING Did you explain why? A 4 looks like: you say why each step, and you test the constant-speed assumption with numbers (challenge B).
3 · COMMUNICATION Can someone follow it? A 4 looks like: organized, units on every number, clear graph, a paragraph a non-expert understands.
4 · TRANSFER TO ASTEROID Did you connect it to the real question? A 4 looks like: you say what we know, what we don't, and why, not just "I did the math."
