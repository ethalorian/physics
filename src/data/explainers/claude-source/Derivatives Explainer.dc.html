AP Physics · Lab notebook 04

Velocity is how fast position changes. Acceleration is how fast velocity changes.

The derivative is the tool that turns "how fast is this changing?" into a number — at a single instant, not averaged over a trip. Apply it once to position and you get velocity. Apply it again and you get acceleration.

The phenomenon

Your car's odometer shows how far you've gone. The speedometer shows how fast you're going right now.

But "right now" lasts zero seconds. How can you divide a distance by zero time?

You can't — but you can shrink the time interval until the answer stops changing. That limit is the derivative, and the speedometer is computing it for you.

01 · From average to instantaneous

Shrink the interval until the answer stops changing

The slope of the line between two points on an x–t graph is the average velocity between them: Δx / Δt. Slide the second point toward the first. The line pivots, and its slope settles on one value — the slope of the tangent line, the velocity at that instant.

04 · Read the chain

Four things the slopes tell you

1
Peaks mean zero.

Wherever x–t has a peak or valley, its tangent is flat, so v = 0. Try the ball toss: at the top, v–t crosses zero.

2
Curvature is acceleration.

x–t bending upward (getting steeper) means a > 0. Bending downward means a < 0. A straight x–t means a = 0.

3
Same sign, speeding up.

When v and a have the same sign, speed grows. Opposite signs, speed shrinks — even if a is positive. Watch the cards under the graphs.

4
Going back up is area.

The reverse of a derivative is the area under the curve. Area under a–t gives Δv; area under v–t gives Δx — the trapezoid from Lab 03.
