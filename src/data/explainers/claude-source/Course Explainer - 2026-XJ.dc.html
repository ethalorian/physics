Antocci Physics · The 2026-XJ series

Something is coming. We have one year of physics to figure out what.

This course follows a single object — a near‑Earth asteroid called 2026‑XJ — from the day it's detected to the day we can finally say what it will do. Every unit of physics answers one more piece of that puzzle.

Planetary defense briefingUpdate 1

A new object, 2026‑XJ, has been detected on an approach toward Earth's neighborhood.

We know roughly where it is and roughly how fast it's moving. We do not yet know enough to say whether it passes or strikes.

That is the point.

This is fiction. The physics — and the equations — are real.
01 · Why build a whole course around one asteroid

A year of physics, one question at a time

1
Physics becomes a tool, not a list.

Most courses teach motion, then forces, then energy, as separate chapters. Here each one shows up because the asteroid story needs it. You learn momentum because you have to know whether a spacecraft could push the asteroid.

2
Every answer opens the next question.

Unit 1 predicts where it's going, assuming it moves in a straight line. It doesn't — gravity bends it. That gap is Unit 2. Each unit ends by naming what it still can't answer.

3
It's how real science works.

Planetary defense teams run this exact loop on real near‑Earth objects: measure, model, predict, state the uncertainty, measure again. The numbers get sharper every update. So do you.

04 · The orbit

Two paths cross. The question is when.

By Unit 2 we know 2026‑XJ orbits the Sun every 1.69 years, and at its closest it dips to 0.98 AU — just inside Earth's orbit. So its path crosses ours twice every lap. Crossing isn't hitting: both have to arrive at the same place at the same time.

05 · Fiction and fact

The asteroid is invented. Nothing else is.

2026‑XJ doesn't exist. But the constants are real, the equations are real, and the method is the one planetary defense teams use on real near‑Earth objects. And one piece of the story already happened.

Fiction
  • The object 2026‑XJ and its specific orbit
  • The seven NASA briefing updates
  • The closing impact odds
Real
  • Every constant: G, g, c, Earth's mass and radius
  • Every equation, from Δx = vt to Kepler's third law
  • Radar tracking, Doppler shifts, TNT‑equivalent yields
  • Tunguska, Chelyabinsk, Chicxulub
Already happened · 2022

NASA's DART spacecraft — about 600 kg at about 6 km/s — deliberately crashed into the small asteroid Dimorphos.

It shortened Dimorphos's orbit by 32 minutes. The math behind it is Unit 3.
