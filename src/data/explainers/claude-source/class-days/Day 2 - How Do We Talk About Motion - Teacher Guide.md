## Stops and answers

**STOP 1 · Warm-up.** Collect answers. Expect "twice the distance" and "zero." Both are right; the next section shows why.

**STOP 2 · Your turn (pairs).** 1 lap: distance 400 m, displacement 0. 1.5 laps: distance 600 m, displacement ≈ 112 m.

> **Correction to the lesson plan:** after 1.5 laps the displacement is **not 200 m**. 200 m is the extra *distance* along half a lap. The displacement is the straight line from the start to the runner's position across the field: ≈ 112 m on a standard 400 m track starting at the finish line (≈ 127 m if the track were a perfect circle). The film shows ≈ 112 m. Good discussion point: "200 m is the path; the displacement is the shortcut."

**STOP 3 · Hands-on.** Students walk 5 steps north, 3 east, 5 south and measure. Answers appear when you continue: distance 13 steps, displacement 3 steps east.

**STOP 4 · Discuss.** They're the same only when you move in a straight line without turning back.

**STOP 5 · Discuss.** Yes. The same 1,000,000 km could bring it closer to Earth, take it farther away, or keep it about the same distance. Answers appear when you continue.

**STOP 6 · Exit ticket.** Look for: distance has no direction, so it can't tell us *where* the asteroid ends up (closer or farther from Earth). Displacement includes direction, which is what decides the outcome.

## Formative checks
- **Exit ticket:** one sentence on why distance traveled isn't enough and displacement is needed.
- **Walking activity:** watch for students struggling with cardinal directions; some may need extra support.
- **Vocabulary:** by the end, can students use "displacement" correctly in a sentence?

The asteroid map is simplified and not to scale (1,000,000 km is tiny compared with 2026-XJ's distance from Earth).
