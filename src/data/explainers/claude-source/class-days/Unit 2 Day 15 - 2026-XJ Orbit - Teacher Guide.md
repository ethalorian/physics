## Answers and what to listen for

**STOP 1 · Group work.**
- Period: T = √(a³) = √(1.8³) = √5.83 ≈ **2.4 years** (T² = a³ in years and AU, around the Sun).
- Perihelion: q = a(1 − e) = 1.8 × 0.52 ≈ **0.94 AU**.
- Aphelion: Q = a(1 + e) = 1.8 × 1.48 ≈ **2.66 AU**.
- Because 0.94 AU < 1 AU < 2.66 AU, the orbit **does reach Earth's orbital distance**.

**STOP 2 · Discuss.** **No**, crossing orbits doesn't mean a collision. The next section animates Earth and 2026-XJ at their real relative rates. On the first pass through Earth's orbit, Earth is about 1.9 AU away; on the next, only about 0.1 AU. Same orbits, very different outcomes: **timing** decides. Students should also name:
1. **Orientation:** real orbits are tilted, so in 3-D the paths may not actually meet (the film's sketch is flat).
2. **Both bodies' positions at the same moment.**
3. **Timing:** is Earth at the crossing point when 2026-XJ is?
4. **Uncertainty** in every measured value.

**STOP 3 · Discuss.** Unit 1 assumed constant velocity: a straight line, fine for days or weeks. Today's model includes gravity, so the path curves and Kepler's laws time the whole orbit, which makes it more trustworthy over months and years. Still missing: tilt, Earth's position over time, and uncertainty.

**STOP 4 · Exit ticket.** Sample: "Perihelion is 0.94 AU (q = a(1 − e)), so the orbit reaches 1 AU. I assumed both orbits lie in the same plane."

**Gather (46–48 min).** Compare group answers; discrepancies usually come from using a instead of q, or from treating "reaches 1 AU" as "hits Earth."

## Formative checks
- Students distinguish **perihelion distance from the Sun** from **distance to Earth**.
- Orbital size and period alone don't establish an encounter: orientation, timing and uncertainty must be stated.
