## Setup notes
- Detector on the floor facing up; ball held about 1 m above it, never closer than ~15 cm (the detector's minimum range). A dense ball (softball, basketball) works better than a tennis ball.
- Sign convention: away from the detector is +, so the falling ball has **negative** velocity and the free-fall line slopes **down** with slope ≈ −g. Students report the magnitude. If your software is set to "toward is +", the line slopes up instead; tell students which convention you're using.

## Answers and what to listen for

**STOP 2 · Discuss.** Three parts: held still (v ≈ 0), the straight falling segment (that's the free fall), and the bounce spike near the detector. It slopes down because the ball moves toward the detector, the negative direction.

**STOP 3 · Lab (15–35 min).** Fit a line to **only** the straight segment. Example demo fit: slope −9.74 m/s² → g ≈ 9.74 m/s².

**Example class data (on screen, no stop).** Four pairs × 3 trials, class average ≈ 9.73 m/s², about 0.8% below 9.81. Replace with your class's data on the board.

**STOP 4 · Sources of error.** Usually the biggest: **selecting the interval** (including bounce or release points) and **detection angle/wobble** (ball drifts out of the cone). Air resistance is small for a dense ball over 1 m and makes measured g slightly *low*. Sample rate matters if only a few points land on the free-fall segment. Ask for a test, e.g. "drop from a different height and see if the slope changes."

**STOP 5 · Individual exit.** Look for: a named free-fall interval, a fitted slope with units, a model (slope of v–t = acceleration), and one assumption (e.g. air resistance negligible). Don't accept "g = 9.81" without their own data.

**STOP 6 · Write-up + Marzano on S4.** Data table + CER paragraph; finish for homework.
