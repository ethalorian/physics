## Answers and what to listen for

**STOP 1 · Predict.** Students sketch each shot landing farther. Push for "what if it's fast enough?" before continuing.

**STOP 2 · Discuss.** **Yes**, it's still falling: gravity pulls it toward Earth the whole way round, so its velocity keeps changing direction. It falls *around*, not *down*: the surface curves away as fast as the ball falls. Listen for students who say "there's no gravity up there" or "it's going too fast to fall."

**STOP 3 · PhET Gravity and Orbits.** Too slow → crash. Just right → circle. Faster → ellipse. Much faster → escape. A circle needs one exact speed for a given height.

**STOP 4 · Your turn.** GMm/r² = mv²/r → v = √(GM/r) = √(3.98 × 10¹⁴ / 6.77 × 10⁶) ≈ **7,670 m/s**, matching Day 12's 7,700 m/s. The satellite's mass cancels. Bigger r → slower orbital speed.

**STOP 5 · Exit ticket.** A strong answer: the ISS *is* falling (gravity pulls it toward Earth), but it also moves sideways at about 7.7 km/s, so as it falls the surface curves away beneath it and it never gets closer. Gravity changes the direction of its velocity, not its speed.

## Formative checks
- A curved free-fall path and an inward gravitational force in the sketch.
- The explanation connects changing velocity direction to acceleration, even at constant speed.
