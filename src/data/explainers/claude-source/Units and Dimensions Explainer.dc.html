AP Physics · Lab notebook 02

A number without a unit isn't a measurement. It's a guess.

Every quantity in physics is a number times a unit, and every unit is built from a handful of basic dimensions. Learn to read them and equations start telling you what they mean before you calculate anything.

The phenomenon · 1999

NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter arrived after a nine‑month flight — and vanished into the Martian atmosphere.

One team's software reported thruster impulse in pound‑force·seconds. The other team read it as newton·seconds. Off by a factor of 4.45.

Every number was computed correctly. The units weren't. That mistake cost $327 million and a spacecraft.

01 · Physical quantities

Three dimensions build all of mechanics

A dimension is the kind of thing you're measuring — length, mass, time. A unit is the agreed‑on amount of it you count with — meters, kilograms, seconds. Mechanics needs only three dimensions. Everything else — speed, force, energy, pressure — is a combination of them.

Lmeter · m
Length

How far. Defined today by the distance light travels in 1/299 792 458 s — so a meter is the same in every lab on Earth.

Mkilogram · kg
Mass

How much stuff — and how hard it is to accelerate. Since 2019, fixed by Planck's constant rather than a metal cylinder in a vault near Paris.

Tsecond · s
Time

How long. One second is 9 192 631 770 oscillations of a cesium‑133 atom — the most precisely realized unit we have.

02 · Base units vs. derived units

Every derived unit is a recipe

A base unit is defined directly: m, kg, s. A derived unit is a product of powers of base units — nothing more. Some get their own name (newton, joule, watt) because we use them constantly, but the name is a nickname for the recipe. Set the exponents and see what you've built.

04 · Why units matter

Units aren't bookkeeping. They're physics.

1
They make numbers mean something.

"The car is going 30" is not information. 30 m/s is highway speed; 30 km/h is a school zone. The Mars orbiter was lost by people who all had the right numbers.

2
Both sides of an equation must match.

You can't set a length equal to a time, or add a force to an energy. This rule — dimensional homogeneity — lets you reject a wrong formula in ten seconds, before you touch a calculator.

3
They tell you what an equation is for.

An answer in kg·m/s² is a force — whatever you called it. An answer in J/s is a power. Units are the fastest way to tell whether you've computed the thing you were asked for.

Habit 1 · Aim at the target unit

Stuck? Write down the unit of the answer, then look at what you have.

Want: s  ·  Have: 20 m and 9.8 m/s²
m ÷ (m/s²) = s² → √ → s
t = √(2·20/9.8) ≈ 2.0 s
Habit 2 · Guess the form of a law

Air drag on a falling object depends on air density ρ, speed v and area A. What's the formula?

[F] = M L T⁻²   [ρ] = M L⁻³   [v] = L T⁻¹   [A] = L²
Only ρ carries M → ρ¹. Only v carries T → v².
Then L: −3 + 2 + 2a = 1 → A¹.
F ∝ ρ v² A — right up to a number.
Habit 3 · Multiply by one

Converting units is multiplying by fractions equal to 1, arranged so the old unit cancels.

72 km/h × (1000 m / 1 km) × (1 h / 3600 s)
= 72 × 1000 / 3600 m/s
= 20 m/s
WHAT UNITS CAN DO
Reject wrong formulas. Predict the form of unknown ones. Tell you what you've computed.
WHAT THEY CAN'T
Find pure numbers — the 2π, the ½, the 4.45. Those need real physics or a measurement.
