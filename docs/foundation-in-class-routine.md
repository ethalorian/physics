# Foundation discussions in class

**For newly formed groups.** The physics goal is for each student to make a claim, test it against another idea, and explain what the evidence supports. The whiteboard is where the conversation happens. The app keeps a short record of each student's thinking. A private reflection may defend the original idea; changing your mind is not required.

## At the start of class

Make tables of two to four. One person creates the group code; classmates join on their own devices. Start when two are ready. Someone arriving late can join a started table with an open seat. Each student can save their first idea in a group that is still forming. If a student has no group for the entire lesson, use the existing teacher-approved independent route. Slides and individual physics questions stay available while they wait.

If a student creates a code by mistake, they can select **Cancel unused code** and immediately create or join another group. If classmates have already joined, **Leave this group** releases only that student’s seat. The code closes when the last student leaves. A first idea saved during setup stays private. At the new table, its author can reuse it as a draft, check it against their new prompt, and decide whether to share it. Students can leave on their own until the group starts.

## If a student is at the wrong table after the group starts

The student opens **Wrong table? Ask to switch**, enters the code shown at the intended table, and sends the request. In the Foundation assignment’s student roster, the teacher selects the student, checks both table codes, and chooses **Approve table switch** or **Decline table switch**. The student can work at the original table or cancel the request while waiting.

Approval moves a student between stops right away. If they have already shared an idea at an unfinished stop, the app holds a seat; saving their private reflection finishes that stop and moves them automatically. The student sees their new table code and completed stops after refreshing. The original table keeps the ideas shared there, the new table sees only work shared with it, and private reflections remain private. Individual answers are unchanged. The teacher’s submitted record shows which stops were finished at a previous table.

Both tables must use the current routine and belong to the same assignment. The new table must have an open seat and a classmate still working. A declined or canceled request leaves the student at the original table. If the new table closes before the move, the student keeps their work and can request another table.

## At each of the three stops

| Beat | What the table does | What each student keeps |
|---|---|---|
| Think first | Give everyone quiet time. A student can sketch or rehearse in a familiar language before entering a short idea. | An individual claim entered before seeing any classmate’s idea. |
| Compare at the board | The lead invites someone to point to a measurement, a force arrow, or another piece of evidence. The scribe draws it. Invite a voice that has not been heard, and rotate the roles at the next stop. | Choose one actual shared idea and explain what its evidence supports or misses. Someone else’s reply is not needed to proceed. |
| Reflect privately | Ask: “What did you keep or change, and why?” Give the table time to listen even if some members have finished typing. | A private explanation in the student’s own words. After all three stops and the individual questions, that student can submit. |

**If someone is delayed:** keep working on the next individual question. A peer can join the discussion after others have begun. If a started group has no other posted idea after 90 seconds from the student's own contribution, they can choose the lesson's example for that stop. Nothing completes automatically. They must critique the example and reflect; the teacher sees its source. The next stop offers real classmates first again. Do not use this route as evidence of peer collaboration or hands-on lab work.

## Try it on Day 16 with net force

Use the lesson’s 5 kg box. Draw a 30 N push to the right and a 10 N force to the left. Each student predicts the acceleration direction. The lead asks, “Which way does the box accelerate? Show us how both arrows support your answer.” The scribe marks the positive axis. One student may start by subtracting the forces. Another may redraw the arrows tip to tail. Both should find a net force of 20 N right and an acceleration of 4 m/s² right. For the private reflection, ask, “Which part of someone else’s drawing or calculation helped you check your own?” Use the individual exit to see whether the reasoning carries over to a different box.

**After the first week:** note where someone waited without doing physics, whether every table member had an idea considered aloud, and whether private explanations cite something specific from the board or example. If fast writers repeatedly choose one another, have the lead invite the quieter student to explain a diagram first at the next stop. If the example becomes the usual path at a table, check group setup and pacing.

## Scope

The routine applies to new groups across the shared Foundation lessons. Existing groups keep the protocol they started with. Earlier submissions, teacher approval for full-lesson make-up, individual lab evidence, mastery decisions, and the foundation → review → quiz prerequisites are preserved.
