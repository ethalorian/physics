## Answers and what to listen for

**STOP 1 · Predict.** Kepler found the orbit is **not** a perfect circle and the Sun is **not** at the centre: it's an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. (Historical note on screen: laws 1 and 2 in 1609, law 3 in 1619, before Newton's *Principia*, 1687.)

**Law 1 (on screen).** Ellipse, foci, centre, perihelion (closest), aphelion (farthest). The two focus-to-planet lines always add to the same length. The ellipse is drawn stretched (e = 0.6); real planet orbits are nearly circular (Earth e = 0.017).

**STOP 2 · Discuss.** Fastest at **perihelion**, slowest at **aphelion**. Law 2 shows why: equal areas in equal times, so a short, wide sector near the Sun needs more distance along the orbit per second.

**Law 3 (on screen).** T² ∝ a³ only for orbits around the **same central body**. Around the Sun in years and AU: T² = a³ (Earth 1 = 1; Jupiter 11.86² = 140.7 ≈ 5.20³ = 140.6). Around Earth, same law, different constant: Moon a = 384,400 km, T = 27.3 days vs ISS a = 6,770 km, T = 93 min; a is 57× larger, so T should be 57 × √57 ≈ 430× longer; actual ≈ 423×.

**STOP 3 · Your turn.** Mars: T = √(1.52³) = √3.51 ≈ **1.87 years** (measured 1.88).

**STOP 4 · Practice.** Earth: 1 year. Mars: 1.87 years. Asteroid a = 2.8 AU: T = √(2.8³) = √22.0 ≈ **4.7 years**.

**STOP 5 · Exit ticket.** Sample: (1) Orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus. (2) A planet sweeps equal areas in equal times, so it moves faster when it's closer to the Sun. (3) For orbits around the same body, the period squared is proportional to the semi-major axis cubed: bigger orbits take much longer.
