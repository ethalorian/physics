## Stops and answers

**STOP 1 · Today's question.** For the constant-velocity walk (0.5 m/s), the v–t graph is a horizontal line at 0.5 m/s. It appears when you continue.

**STOP 2 · Your turn.** Steep → high horizontal line. Shallow → low horizontal line. Flat → a line on v = 0. Toward the sensor → horizontal line below zero. Answers appear when you continue, plus a speeding-up example (tilted line).

**STOP 3 · Discuss.** Fastest constant motion: the highest horizontal line (steep x–t). The toward-sensor line is below zero because that motion is in the negative direction (position decreasing). The height of a v–t line is the velocity; its sign is the direction.

**STOP 4 · Your turn.** On one scale (1 square = 25 mph): car → 2.4 squares east; plane → 8 squares north; runner 5 m/s ≈ 11 mph → under half a square, southwest (45° between S and W). Check for proportional length **and** correct direction.

**STOP 5 · Your turn (graph paper).** Students draw 8,500 m/s at 15° to scale (e.g. 1 cm = 1,000 m/s → 8.5 cm), drop a perpendicular, and measure: ≈ 8.2 cm toward Earth, ≈ 2.2 cm perpendicular. Trig check: v cos 15° ≈ 8,210 m/s, v sin 15° ≈ 2,200 m/s. Today is visual; protractors tomorrow.

**STOP 6 · Your turn.** Toward Earth: 8.21 km/s × 1.6 × 10⁷ s ≈ 1.3 × 10⁸ km. Perpendicular: 2.20 km/s × 1.6 × 10⁷ s ≈ 3.5 × 10⁷ km. Answers appear when you continue.

**STOP 7 · Exit ticket.** Look for: the toward-Earth part tells us whether (and how fast) it closes in on Earth (could it hit?); the perpendicular part tells us where it will be when it gets here. Both are needed to predict its position.

## Formative checks
- **Vector drawings:** proportional length AND correct direction. Both matter.
- **Exit ticket:** toward-Earth → will it hit; perpendicular → where it will be when it arrives.
- **Watch for:** students who can do cos/sin but can't say why. The meaning is the physics, not the math.
