## Using each stop
**STOP 1 · Discuss.** After highlighting one or two strong transfer-task predictions. Aim for "something pulls on it": a force changes velocity (Unit 1: F = ma).

**Demo.** Run it live before or alongside the film: hardcover book and a flat sheet from the same height, same release; then crumple the same sheet and repeat. The film shows both drops so students can replay them.

**STOP 2 · Predict.** Get commitments before the crumpled drop. Many students predict the book still wins "because it's heavier."

**STOP 3 · Discuss.** The paper's mass didn't change, only its shape. Air resistance on the flat sheet is large; on the ball it's small. Without air, all objects fall with the same acceleration. Listen for students separating *air resistance* from *gravitational acceleration*.

**STOP 4 · Notice & Wonder (18–28 min).** Students fill both boxes, then sort wonders into "physics can answer" and "physics can't." Keep the list: it seeds later lessons.

**Mini-lecture preview (no stop).** Gravity is a force between any two masses and it follows a law (Newton's universal gravitation). Don't derive it today; frame the unit question: does 2026-XJ's path bend enough to bring it to Earth?

**STOP 5 · Exit ticket.** Look for gravity as an interaction that changes velocity: mainly the Sun's pull (Earth's too) bends the path. No net force → constant velocity (Day 9); a net force → changing velocity.

**STOP 6 · Marzano check-in on K1 / R4.** Day 1, so low ratings are expected; ask what they'd need to move up one level.

## Next teaching move
Sort exit responses by misconception: "heavier falls faster," "gravity only on Earth," "nothing pulls in space." Start next class with a short diagram or comparison for those groups, then a new example to check whether their reasoning changed.
