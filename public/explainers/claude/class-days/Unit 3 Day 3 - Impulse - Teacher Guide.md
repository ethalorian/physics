## Answers and what to listen for

**STOP 1 · Quick check.** p = mv, units **kg·m/s**, a **vector** (same direction as the velocity).

**Derivation (on screen).** F = ma, and a = Δv/Δt, so F = mΔv/Δt. Multiply by Δt: FΔt = mΔv = Δp. Name FΔt the **impulse J**. Units: N·s = (kg·m/s²)·s = kg·m/s, the same as momentum.

**STOP 2 · Your turn.** East = +. Δp = (0.15)(−50) − (0.15)(+40) = −7.5 − 6.0 = −13.5 kg·m/s. F = Δp/Δt = −13.5 ÷ 0.002 = **−6,750 N**, i.e. **6,750 N west** (toward the pitcher). Watch for students who drop the sign or use 10 m/s for Δv.

**Area idea (on screen).** Impulse is the area under a force–time graph. A short, hard push and a long, gentle push with the same area give the same Δp; that's tomorrow's "why it matters."

**STOP 3 · Group practice.** Five mixed problems: some give F and Δt (find Δp), some give Δp and Δt (find F).

**STOP 4 · Exit ticket.** J = FΔt = (8 × 10⁵ N)(0.05 s) = **4 × 10⁴ N·s = 4 × 10⁴ kg·m/s**, in the direction of the push (the impactor's direction of travel).

**A note on DART's real numbers.** Your plan describes this as DART; the film labels it "classroom numbers" because the real impact was much larger. DART (about 580 kg at about 6.1 km/s) carried roughly 3.5 × 10⁶ kg·m/s, and debris blasted off the surface added more: published estimates put the total momentum transferred at about 3–4 times the spacecraft's own. The exit-ticket numbers are fine as a calculation; if a student asks, that's a great extension question.

**STOP 5 · Marzano on K2 and S2.** Tomorrow: why does impulse matter beyond the math?

## Formative checks
- Impulse described as a change in momentum, with N·s equivalent to kg·m/s.
- Direction of the change checked, not just its size.

## Next teaching move
Sort exit tickets for missing direction, unit confusion (N vs N·s), or F × Δt mixed up with F ÷ Δt. Open next class with the two same-area pulses side by side and ask which one an egg would survive.
